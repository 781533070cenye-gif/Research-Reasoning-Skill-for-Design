# Calibrated examples

Use these examples to calibrate behavior, not as fixed content. They intentionally draw on design and museum research because they expose common boundary errors, but the reasoning transfers to other domains.

## 1. Early intuition

**User:** “虚拟博物馆明明不受建筑限制，为什么还是总做得像一座实体馆？这能成为研究吗？”

**Poor response:** Immediately supplies a grand title, selects an experiment, and claims a research gap.

**Better response:** Route to `CRYSTALLIZE`. Identify the phenomenon (digital environments reproduce building-like navigation), the default assumption (museum legibility or authority depends on spatial resemblance), the tension (technical freedom coexists with representational conservatism), and several explanations. State what would need literature verification. Produce two or three researchable versions distinguished by scale, without pretending one is already final.

## 2. Significant interaction

**User:** “Situation × strategy is significant. Can I say visitors dynamically change their learning behavior?”

**Poor response:** “Yes, the interaction proves learning behavior is dynamic.”

**Better response:** Route to `EVIDENCE`. A rating interaction supports systematic differences in reported strategy ratings across situations. It does not alone show enacted behavior, within-person temporal change during a visit, or learning outcomes. Inspect whether the design is repeated-measures and whether individual reconfiguration was analyzed. A defensible claim may concern situation-dependent strategy configuration; stronger behavioral or learning claims need other evidence.

## 3. Movement traces and learning

**User:** “Visitors revisit and move backward, so they are learning through reflection.”

**Poor response:** Rewrites the sentence more academically.

**Better response:** Diagnose an evidence-boundary problem. Revisit and backward movement are observed spatial behaviors. Reflection is an interpretation; learning is an outcome not established by movement alone. Offer a level-matched sentence and specify what additional data—think-aloud, recall, comprehension, interview, or linked interaction traces—could test the stronger interpretation.

## 4. Multi-study synthesis

**User:** “Study 1 observes movement transitions, and Study 2 measures strategy ratings. How do I say they validate each other?”

**Poor response:** Forces a one-to-one mapping between movement categories and self-reported strategies.

**Better response:** Build a synthesis map. The studies may contribute evidence at different scales to a shared problem: whether visitor activity is better modeled as situated and revisable than as a stable profile. Use complementary language unless the measures and design establish direct validation. Preserve each study's `cannot show` boundary.

## 5. Attractive new concept

**User:** “Can I call this situated reconfiguration?”

**Poor response:** Celebrates the phrase and inserts it throughout the manuscript.

**Better response:** Run a Concept Card: definition, empirical referents, distinction from adaptation or strategy switching, non-examples, closest literature, and evidence level. Keep the term as `Proposed` until the chain from observed patterns to construct is explicit.

## 6. Submission-stage new idea

**User:** “The paper is due tomorrow, but I suddenly think institutional authority could be the real story.”

**Poor response:** Rewrites the introduction and discussion around institutional authority.

**Better response:** Preserve `CONVERGE`. Put the idea in `Next study` or `Side project`, then ask whether the current paper becomes invalid without it. Reopen the conceptual layer only if the existing argument materially misrepresents the evidence.

## 7. Design implication

**User:** “Our findings mean museums should deploy an AI guide that detects visitor state.”

**Poor response:** Elaborates features and architecture immediately.

**Better response:** Derive the implication in layers. First ask which static design assumption changes. Then identify the needed capacity, such as revisable and uncertainty-aware support. Finally examine whether AI sensing is necessary, proportionate, consented to, and better than nontechnical alternatives. Treat a system proposal as future design work unless evaluated.

## 8. General-domain transfer

**User:** “Interviewees used three coping patterns, so there are three user types.”

**Poor response:** Names the types and suggests personas.

**Better response:** Trigger the state-versus-type check. Ask whether the same participant used multiple patterns, whether context predicted switching, and whether persistence was measured. Frame provisional configurations or strategies if stability is not established.
